INTERVIEW WITH WOUT VANDEVENNE (°2001)

Hilde Van Canneyt (HVC): Dear Wout, you just graduated from LUCA in Ghent. Have you set up a studio somewhere yet?

Wout Vandevenne (WV): I live in Aalst and found a studio in Zwalm, about half an hour's drive away. I'm combining this with an Educational Master's program. It's a period of searching.

HVC: I assume you have to schedule those studio hours well?

WV At least in blocks of five hours. By the way, sitting in the studio for eight hours is too long. You’re just not productive then.

HVC: How did painting come into your life?

WV I first studied architecture at Sint-Lucas, and when we had art history, I made the connection: I wanted to feel that freedom during creation as well. However, I couldn't necessarily paint well, and to this day, I still feel that way, which is why I combine it with drawing.

HVC: Most of your drawings are – compared to your paintings – small in size. (A4) Do you specifically go to your studio for that, or can you also manage that with a sketchbook at the kitchen table? Because they are not typical 'quick sketches'.

WV No, I really see them as drawings. But I always have the feeling that paintings are viewed with more 'wow'! I always feel more freedom while drawing, as if I have a better grip on it. In painting, there are so many possibilities; it seems like a larger journey of discovery. That's why it's also a significant step to start a painting. They measure 1.70m by 1.20m. I think I only made one painting in the last few months, and the rest are drawings, but on larger formats, even up to A1. Now that I know I have an exhibition at NQ gallery, I'm working towards that, but that can also block me, of course.

HVC: You like to depict your 'own' world versus the 'big' world. But there's more: it's 'your' reality combined with your memory.

WV I used to work more towards reality; I feel that this has loosened up more now.

HVC: The narrative – almost storytelling – is important; each image has a kind of past and future.

WV For the upcoming expo at NQ, I have, so to speak, started a play in which I invented various characters. I usually draw from my imagination; before, I often worked from photos that I first sketched out with a pen.

HVC: Your work also revolves around existential loneliness, which is, by the way, ingrained in most people. Philippe Vandenberg once said that there is little or no art about happiness. 'Humans don't need that; it's about the flaw in the blues of human existence.' Do you agree?

WV I try not to specify that too much because otherwise, I feel pinned down by it. I don't know how my work will evolve, what I'll do next year... Of course, I do notice that they are usually separate figures. But this is not intentional.

HVC: In the expo (expo)nentieel – of which I am the curator – at the Nodenaysteen in Ghent, you show the work Seeing double. Two figure portraits, each looking in the opposite direction.

WV I see that as two figures that are stuck, but on the other hand, each has a different perspective.

HVC: Do you think about what you will make on your way to your studio?

WV Nowadays, I am inspired by strange uniforms and Dadaist figures. While before, I placed characters from my personal circle in a specific setting. Before I start, I do have an image in mind; I might sketch it out briefly and put it into a narrative. It's strange, but I usually get ideas at random moments. I then try to visualize them. Once I work it out, I see where it ends up.

HVC: Luc Tuymans never has a realistic image in his head, but rather a 'mood'. He then completes the final formulation in a day.

WV When drawing, there is more directness; the colors are also more direct and intense. In painting, you have and see that layering, which also affects the 'freshness.' I still need to master that directness and intensity in my paintings. But I don't value painting more than drawing.

HVC: Your works have a comic-like quality; on one hand, there is simplicity and stillness, sometimes even melancholy, while on the other hand, there is absurdity, like in 'The Prayer of Doubt.'

WV Lately, I have been leaning towards the strange. For example, I saw a work by James Ensor featuring a puppet figure in a typical uniform, as well as Caravaggio with one of his works where someone is being bitten on the neck. That fits together for me, and that's why I create a work from it. The fact that it references art history is an added bonus. With The Prayer of Doubt, I refer to the Flemish primitives; those typical figures from the Burgundian period. Then I wonder if I can place them in a contemporary setting. It's also a bit of a joke with art history because, let's face it, it is taken very seriously. (winks) But of course, I know I should be grateful for our rich art history. I find it crazy how, as an artist, you can combine all these ingredients. Some works flow into the stage, like that piece with the view from the window – Shadow of the Night – which then continues into a smaller work. Everything is a fragment of a larger story.

HVC: In terms of technique, figuration, and content, you are influenced by Jean Brusselmans, Gustave De Smet, and James Ensor.

WV Philip Guston also speaks to me a lot, even though my work doesn't resemble his.

HVC: Do you see yourself bringing typical work in 2024? I find you also expose yourself with your naive imagery.

WV I find it difficult to answer that...

HVC: Are you aware that you still have a long journey in art ahead of you? Are you willing to make sacrifices for it?

WV I just need to create those things; this is who I am.

HVC: For whose career would you like to draw?

WV Richard Diebenkorn and David Park appeal to me. Although I don't know their lives, I am content enough with my own life. (winks) But I only interpret; I shouldn't compare myself to other artists, or else I’ll go crazy.

What I find a difficult balance is life and family versus work and how to make ends meet, to combine everything, etc. And should I think commercially or not, because that would allow me to fund my materials. I find it a tough consideration. And when you are working on your art, you are in your own world. Is that positive or negative?

HVC: That is literally me-time, claiming your own time. You do it for yourself, but ultimately you want to engage, even receive applause.

What do you hope for the viewer when they see your work hanging in the exhibition at the NQ gallery?

WV I simply enjoy being able to share the works; that coming together is important to me. Although, at openings, I feel it often revolves more around the gathering of people. Sometimes I find that a bit too much. (winks) HVC Ah, both are necessary! Introspection versus the social (winks)

By the way, I still can't quite put my finger on your work. It's not Gefundenes Fressen. But I see that as a positive thing. Hilde Van Canneyt Wout Vandevenne is exhibiting:

19.01.2025 – 25.03.2025
