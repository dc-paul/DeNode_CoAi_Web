Interview with Stijn Cole

Hilde Van Canneyt
AI translation

Hilde Van Canneyt (HVC): Sint-Amandsberg, November 2008.

HVC: I meet the versatile artist Stijn Cole (b. 1978) in his cozy studio in Sint-Amandsberg.

HVC: Hello Stijn. You create videos, sculptures, models, installations, mixed media, and paintings. How did you come to work so broadly across different media to tell your story?

Stijn Cole (SC): I graduated in advertising and then in sculpture. The choice of different media is because I find it limiting to pin myself down to one medium to tell my story. The content of my work revolves around the same horizon. I choose the medium that fits the work at that moment. For me, it's a hindrance to only think in terms of canvas or to work in 3D. Since 2005, for example, I haven't made any sculptures. If I do again, it will be because it's necessary for the work in question, but right now I have no need for it. The works in the Witte Zaal gained added value by painting them, so I worked with that medium. I think a medium should be a conceptual choice.

HVC: So you always start from that thinking from which you then determine your medium? Do you never think about working around a different theme?

SC: Well,... it's not that I'm not open to that, but I always end up with an extension of my previous work.

HVC: When did you start with that theme of landscape, the horizon? Had you been working on that for a long time? What fascinates you about it?

SC: I actually don't want to see my works in terms of the theme of landscape. It's more about light. How each object or external form depends on the light that falls on it; color depends on the intensity of the light that is reflected. My works are about that. How an image presents itself under certain circumstances and how that depends on the outside light.

HVC: In the sculpture training, we were quite conceptually guided. There were artists who took refuge in what they experienced themselves, but in my case, I didn't find that very interesting. Others were politically or socially engaged, but that didn't resonate with me either. So I took a subject that everyone sees and is constantly confronted with, and that every form of art is about: how does an object present itself and how do you experience that? I wanted to purify that until I had a message left. My contribution is very minimal; I purify and I distill until I come up with a good way to clarify an idea. In that way, I capture it. That decision is my contribution. An example is my timelines that are executed photographically. Here I also start from an observation, a registration, and then I throw everything overboard until I reach the essence.

HVC: You do that with your works in two color parts: we see a blue key and a green key (a blue area on a green area) that you reduce to a landscape.

SC: I have used that composition in various places. For example, in Germany, I painted an entire wall in blue and green in a darkened space with the boundary at my eye level. Light patches were then projected onto that part of the wall, making it visible again. I had made a selection of 84 landscapes from masters in art history with an infinite horizon. The light spots were based on that. The horizon is the point where a landscape becomes subjective. Because you perceive a landscape from your own perspective. That translates into the position of the horizon.

HVC: If I want to perceive those painted landscapes in a way that they become windows to reality again, I need to adjust that horizon line in those landscapes to my eye level. I literally did that in the Dhondt-Dhaenens museum. There, I hung some works from the collection with the horizon at my eye level.

HVC: Why that confrontation with those old landscape paintings?

SC: Because the collection of Dhondt-Dhaenens is strongly rooted in the Latem school, where landscape painting held an important place. I wanted to see those paintings again as observational works, as windows. In an observational drawing, the horizon line or eye line serves as the basis on which the rest is built. You never actually perceive a work again as it was created.

HVC: By placing that horizon line back at eye level, the works, in my opinion, come closer to reality. I want to show this to the viewer. And with those green and blue keys in between, also used as background color in TV recordings, you can mentally place any possible image there. In that way, it can be any possible other landscape.

HVC: Light and color are the basic components in your work; do you think you can go on infinitely with that?

SC: No, each work is a continuation of the previous one; I will still refer back to some paths as hooks in certain works or setups, as I have now done with the piece in the hall of the Witte Zaal. Other elements will seep into the new works. It's a bit difficult to predict this already. I do think that in the end, a coherent whole will be formed. Although that is also difficult to predict.

HVC: Your work is linked to minimal art. Are you influenced by that, or what attracted you to it?

SC: It's true that minimal thinking is absolutely not a starting point, and I am actually not influenced by it. I come from a family where the artistic preference leaned towards minimal design; I grew up with it. My preference for forms and visual language does lean towards forms and visual language that are tight and minimal. By purifying and distilling, throwing overboard unnecessary elements, I inevitably arrive at minimal images, but I struggle with that. On the one hand, I want to break away from it, and on the other hand, I don't want to.

HVC: Do you always know what the end result will be before you start a work?

SC: Yes, actually. It is always a long search for what it will become, but the end result is always very clear after a certain point.

HVC: So there is little room for spontaneity?

SC: Well, for the paintings in the Witte Zaal, it was the first time that the result was unknown in advance.

HVC: In the Witte Zaal, we indeed see two paintings for the first time. We see square blocks in different colors derived from a landscape. At the bottom, time and date are indicated. The same date with a 1.5-hour difference in time.

SC: My works always first go through a digital filter. On the computer, I convert colors. This also applies to those paintings. My starting point was hyperrealism. That idea of mimicking an image is something that actually doesn't interest me. Here, I discarded the formal aspect and only kept the color. I applied a filter that happens automatically on the internet. Every image posted on the internet that serves as a window to the world is rendered to 256 colors.

HVC: In this way, I rendered two photos of the same landscape at different moments. Instead of arranging the colors in an order that forms an image, I shaded them from light to dark. Those paintings are actually 256 squares, white at the top and black at the bottom with all the colors in between. Under the influence of the intensity of light, those two landscapes appear completely different.

HVC: So I show a thorough abstraction of the landscape from a conceptual perspective, not from the standpoint of the image.

HVC: A kind of analysis of the landscape?

SC: Indeed, if I had simply shown two photos of a landscape, people would have been stuck in that image. But now you only see colors and no unnecessary formal information.

HVC: And where does the choice of the medium paint come from?

SC: At first, I just wanted to print them, but that outcome was flat and two-dimensional at that size. The act of mixing paint was something I couldn't leave to the computer. My other works are more about executing an idea without much room for deviation. That was not the case with these paintings. I started with full enthusiasm and soon felt that it was my thing. Because you don't have control over those paintings, I also felt for the first time that I 'worked' in my studio. I had no experience with the medium; I am not a painter, and for me, it is not painting. For me, that choice of paint was a conceptual choice. I wanted to give my images a kind of power that I didn't find in another medium for this work.

HVC: These two works also appear much warmer than the cool work we usually see from you. Is that coolness in your works intentional?

SC: My works are - I think - distant because I have banished every personal element from them until now. But with my paintings, I have gone beyond that for the first time. I feel like I have skated on thin ice in relation to my own starting point. Apparently, that is inherent to the medium. The act of painting cannot be seen separately from yourself. My other works remained digital until the result.

HVC: But can you still speak of art if most of your artworks come out of the computer?

SC: Yes, of course. I am the one who makes the choices and comes up with the concepts. The contribution of my personal touch or handwriting actually has no added value, so it doesn't need to be there either.

HVC: What do you want to convey to the viewer with your installation in the Witte Zaal, which consists of two light boxes separated by a wall? In one room, we see green light, called Spring Green, while the other room is bathed in pink light and is named Tulip Pink.

SC: For me, it is a different way of telling the same thing. In those light boxes, you get the base color of a color; this is the illuminated part. The other side is an unlit part, so that color is determined by the light intensity present in the room at that moment. You constantly see how that color presents itself at that moment and how that color serves as a base color. If you were to see that work in a bright environment, there would be no nuance between the two.

HVC: So you want us to pause and reflect on that color intensity of the light?

SC: Indeed. Take a sculpture, in the end, you are not looking at that sculpture but at the shape of that sculpture under the influence of the light in that space. When you see that in a catalog, it is perfectly lit, but when you look at it in the circumstances of that moment, you have to take those circumstances into account. You usually don't realize that because the floor, ceiling, and walls are also influenced by that light.

HVC: Does your work have more aesthetic or symbolic value for you?

SC: At the subsidy committee, they described my work as too formal. But that's not true; it's actually the opposite. The formal aspect is subordinate. The symbolic, conceptual part is important. The aesthetic result is purely a consequence of that concept. If you throw away everything that disturbs, you will get a pure and beautiful image anyway.

HVC: Do you think that when your work is exhibited, the viewer should fill in what you want to convey themselves? Or do you believe that there should be explanatory text to inform us about the what and how of your work?

SC: I see it as a necessity in my work. People sometimes interpret my works differently than I want to convey, and at that moment, I feel they are not looking at my work. There is room for interpretation: for example, with the timelines, the strength of the work lies in the viewer filling in the colors according to their own feelings. But I think it should happen within the lines of my concept.

HVC: Without text, does your work not stand on its own?

SC: The idea that a work should speak for itself is as outdated as it gets. With the baggage I carry, I understand only 4% of what I see. And that 4% is not the most interesting part.

HVC: If you could dream out loud, what projects would you still like to do?

SC: I have a full-time job. I dream of constantly working against deadlines and being able to spend full-time in my studio.

HVC: And you can't do this because you don't receive subsidies?

SC: Among other things. I have about fifty projects in the drawer that I want to execute, but certain things I can only create depending on a space. That's also a reason why I enjoy my paintings so much. I can prepare them and then hang them depending on the space. But with my other works, I really need that space first to work towards, so I need to be invited first.

HVC: How do you see yourself in relation to other contemporary artists? Your work can be linked at first glance to that of Pieter Vermeersch.

SC: I know Pieter and his work, but we are not in each other's waters. Aesthetically, you can link us, but that's where it ends.

HVC: Among the other artists, I don't know how I fit in, because it's been four years since I was last invited to a group exhibition. Maybe I'm not networking enough. I do follow the exhibitions, but not as much as I should.

HVC: Do you want to say anything about the Belgian art scene?

SC: I think the idea of subsidies is completely flawed. There are objective criteria, but they don't count. The system of 1000 files where people rely on the images is totally outdated.

HVC: How do you want to be remembered as an artist later?

SC: I don't know how I will evolve myself, but if I were to drop dead tomorrow, I would like to be remembered for the exhibitions that are currently running.

Stijn, thank you for the honest conversation.
