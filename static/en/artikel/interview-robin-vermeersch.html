Interview with Robin Vermeersch

Hilde Van Canneyt
AI translation

Hilde Van Canneyt (HVC): Interview with Robin Vermeersch Waregem, December 18, 2008.

HVC: Taking the train from Ghent to Waregem. It's something different. At the station, visual artist Robin Vermeersch (°1977) is waiting for me with cake. We enjoy it in his residency at B-Part, where he has a spacious studio for four months.

HVC: Hello Robin. How did you end up here in your residency at B-Part?

Robin Vermeersch (RV): A friend of mine, Hans Demeulenaere, had a residency here two years ago and recommended me to Patrick Ronse, the artistic director and coordinator of Be-Part. Together we looked at my works, and he was open to having me here as a guest. I’m lucky to be able to stay here for four months instead of the usual three months.

HVC: We are now at the end of those four months. What have you ultimately accomplished here?

RV: The goal was to create a large diorama. I have always been fascinated by dioramas. I teach at the DKO and always give assignments involving dioramas because you can create a whole unique world in that small space. In the past, I also made a diorama of two square meters. Now I wanted to repeat that, but instead of white sculptures like in my previous box, these are painted figures. They are panels cut from plywood, which you can compare to set pieces from a classic theater play. These are two-dimensional, but due to the strong light-dark effect, you get the illusion that they are three-dimensional. If I then set them up like a stage set, I create a foreground, middle ground, and background, resulting in a kind of unique world. I wanted to create recognition through certain shapes, but at the same time, a unique organism emerges. That was my intention.

HVC: Normally, I am not good at developing predetermined concepts and sticking to them. I don’t like planning. The only thing I knew was the atmosphere I wanted to create. This residency is a good moment to adhere to my starting point.

HVC: Did you first paint the cut-out boards randomly and then try to fit them into the diorama?

RV: Indeed, I cut and painted those boards without considering the diorama at first, just as I felt. That box has been empty here since September. I even considered leaving my box empty and letting my painted boards stand loose in the space. Then I started with one board, and before I knew it, most of the boards were already puzzling together in the box. And I think they belong there now. Although I also find it interesting to leave my painted panels loose in the space and show the construction of the back, otherwise it seems like I only want to show the clean front of the panels. But ultimately, I realize that if you start around a clear idea and work towards it, you have to follow that, because my painted panels apparently do not work as independent sculptures. In the box, they work better because I made those panels to merge into a whole, and they apparently need the enclosure of the box. Now, after three days of looking at my finished diorama, I feel that it works.

HVC: Would it have become a different artwork if you had worked piece by piece towards your diorama from the beginning with your cut-out painted boards?

RV: Yes, sometimes I need to be contrary, to go against myself and to completely dismantle my works first. Now it has become a stronger work because I wasn’t consciously focused on the construction of my diorama and could then puzzle and play with my panels, just like you build a painting.

HVC: Why did you paint those boards in your typical organic form language?

RV: I didn’t want to represent anything with that, but within my form language, you can recognize shapes that resemble stones, plants, shells… I rework those shapes into new organic images that I then paint in a strong light-dark contrast with pastel-like soft colors. It is not the intention for you to recognize concrete forms in them.

HVC: How does working here in B-Part differ from working in your own studio? A fresh empty studio must have some influence, right? Your own studio is usually filled with stuff, clutter, and distractions?

RV: I indeed found it good that I entered an empty space with only that diorama annex cube that I had placed here. In my own studio, everything is indeed scattered around, and I work on everything simultaneously. Here, I was forced to finish something as well. I wanted to be physically present here and saw the end of those four months as a deadline for myself.

HVC: At Locuslux, I unfortunately stood in front of a closed door on a cold Sunday afternoon, but I did see some of your works through the large window. Tell me what I missed up close.

RV: I have a group exhibition there with Marc Nagtzaam and Boris Thibaut, curated by Jan Denys. I am showing new colored pencil drawings, along with a series of wall sculptures and a larger sculpture on a pedestal. My new drawings are fields with all small cells that form a certain rhythm, each cell is different in shape. The organic and light-dark contrast are really palpable. In the past, you could still discover tunnels or landscapes in my drawings, now I want to delve deeper into it, pressing into a form like a seam. You now also see more repetition of the same structures, but all structures are different. I drew my inspiration from microscopy.

HVC: On the wall hang about twenty wall sculptures. That idea is based on the shapes you see on a climbing wall. Although I feel less like a painter and more like a sculptor, I have always been extremely fascinated by wall sculptures and the wall shadows they cast.

HVC: The large sculpture is a tunnel, born from a drawing. The idea of an inside and an outside of a shape fascinated me. When you look inside, you see a kind of cave structure and enter another world; from the outside, you see an elongated abstract shape. At first, I placed a white pedestal under it, but that was too harsh; the wooden pedestal fits better with this work, it is rudimentary and warm.

HVC: When we look at your black-and-white drawings on your website, we enter a world of grids, spirals, curves, pipes, corridors, and mazes, mostly in parallel black-and-white lines. Where does your fascination for those curves and such come from? How did you start working like that?

RV: I started with abstracted portraits that I slowly built up using a hatching technique with pen and ink, in which I was searching for the tension of the portrait within the sheet. I wanted to create a layering effect with the multiple hatching. I didn't want the drawings to have a fleeting character; there needed to be a tangible hand in them, they had to feel charged. I began this series of drawings in 2000 and stopped in 2001.

HVC: In 2005, I resumed that technique and started with one A4-sized drawing to bring some structure to my mind. The images that emerged were literally brain twists; later, I began to work more linearly. Those A4 drawings spontaneously arose from, for example, one dot; others were pre-conceived and sketched out with pencil. Through this series of drawings, I regained a connection with my visual language, which I had somewhat lost before.

HVC: How do you know where such an image begins or ends?

RV: It grows organically; I feel it at a certain moment. Sometimes I see an image in it and continue from there, starting to add structure.

HVC: Your drawings aren't exactly cheerful; they have something dark about them.

RV: They are dark, but not morbid; they show a bit of the 'underbelly.' I have that in myself as well. I try to scrape away that layer, and you can see that in those images too. I start from within myself and call them 'images from the other side.' But there is always a possibility of escape, as if I want to show a piece of a world that lies much further behind.

HVC: Your paintings, on the other hand, are more colorful, have fewer twists, and seem more playful. Is that your 'other side' as well?

RV: I think so; I never want to consciously go deep and dark. Sometimes it’s literally a counterbalance that I work with light forms and colors. But even in my diorama with those light colors, I try to insert a secretly dark side with my shadows. But I am aesthetically inclined; the forms have to be beautiful.

HVC: In addition to your bas-reliefs and your tunnel, you once made an epoxy star-shaped sculpture called 'Big sculpture.' We see all these tunnels converging, drawing you in. Where did that idea come from?

RV: It originated from a drawing of different funnels. There are various funnels with different entrances and exits. They all lead to the same center, as if there is no other way, where everything comes together at one point. I also present this work simply on the ground, as if it is just lying there and the viewer has to bend down to look into it, only to find that it leads nowhere... Outside, you see a rough shape, but inside, a very refined form. Again, inside versus outside. I am fascinated by tunnels and connections, the childlike idea of 'what's around the corner'?

HVC: How do you determine which material to work with when you have such a sculpture in mind?

RV: I work with epoxy because it is an easy material to work with. Although all my sculptures are first sculpted in clay, and then I make molds from them. Clay can be strongly manipulated, and you can easily add and remove pieces. This way, I can quickly apply my thinking to it. But I don’t want to exhibit in that material. When you fire clay, I find it looks too much like ceramics, and I don’t want to convey that feeling in my larger sculptures.

HVC: You also once made a diorama called 'The Barricade.' We see worm-like sculptures that seem to emerge from the ground. Tell me about it!

RV: When you come to the museum in Tervuren, you see old display cases with, for example, stuffed tigers that all have their own atmosphere. I was fascinated by the idea that you can encapsulate something, look into it, and are forced to look in one way, so that you have the illusion of being in that other world. From there, I created that work. Those 'worm-like' sculptures come from a double bottom because I wanted to suggest sculptures that grow. As if they would have grown a bit further by the next visit, and the sculpture, just like in real life, is always in motion.

HVC: With your wax landscape...

RV: ...the same idea was at play. Pieces that emerge from somewhere... as if a piece from another world, another planet. The image grew from the idea that, when it snows and starts to melt, you sometimes see that some pieces reappear. Then you have those little bits that are visible in a large white field.

HVC: You have done 'window art' several times. What do you want to achieve with that for passersby?

RV: The first time was for the Kunstencentrum Croxhapox. They have been providing an 'art window' for artists in the Onderstraat in Ghent for some time. I have always enjoyed doing things within the city, outside the framework of an exhibition space. I wanted to create two display cases. In the city, everything is very strict, and at that moment, I needed nature; I constantly wondered what I was actually doing in the city. I wanted to show immediately engaging forms that work both during the day and at night. The idea of people passing by and thinking: 'What is this?' It evokes something, but you also don’t spontaneously think: 'This is art.' I’m not fond of that distance in art. I also don’t think many people will say that about my work because they do have a certain connection with that visual language; they won’t immediately reject it.

HVC: At Overpoort in Kunstzicht, you had 'window on the world,' a large board where artists could do their thing. I started by placing eleven dots, like a formation of a football team. I went there every two days for three months and added all possible running lines of the football players. In the end, you have a kind of spatial structure within a street that actually doesn’t belong there. I found that fascinating, that a student who daily has their meal in the restaurant of Overpoort literally sees the work change every day.

HVC: So you also like to create art for the common man who knows nothing about art?

RV: I do notice that there are people who know nothing about art who appreciate my work, and I find that nice. I don't want them to not understand it and have to say: explain it to me. Of course, I like that it is appreciated by both the low and the high, although I won't go broke over it.

HVC: Your work indeed doesn't require text and explanation, unlike many other contemporary artists.

RV: It is a visual work, so I believe it should stand on its own.

HVC: Do you think this should also apply to the visual work of your colleagues?

RV: No, I think some artists work differently and are therefore harder to understand for the average person. But that doesn't make it hermetic art. I can enjoy a work by Donald Judd, but I can imagine that his work speaks less immediately to a layperson. I aim to create tactile work so that my work appeals for what it is.

HVC: You grew up with a father who was an artist. How should I imagine that? Four little brothers drawing on their father's lap?

RV: Not at all! But we did grow up with art and beauty around us. And not only my father, Rik Vermeersch, but my mother was and is also very creative, always painting. Although I can't imagine for a moment that our father was some kind of teacher for us at the table. He was mainly focused on his own work and thought it was nice that we all drew, but as a child, I was actually more into playing football than drawing. Still, I had my own world in my head, even though I wasn't the great artist among the brothers. I do remember that when we went to visit my grandfather José Vermeersch every Sunday, we would all be drawing together at a big table at the end of the day. My dad also made very large sculptures out of chicken wire and plaster, and as kids, we would recreate that in his studio. Or our parents would take us to openings and exhibitions.

HVC: But specifically, I learned a lot from my father's library friends. Later, we were also free to do what we wanted. I was torn between being a farmer or an artist, but those art books had bitten me with everything that was art history. I was about 15 at the time. I really don't think you should underestimate the influences from home.

HVC: Your older brothers Tinus and Pieter are also no strangers in the contemporary art world. As the youngest, did you never feel like rebelling against your older brothers and your father?

RV: During my high school years, I never felt that, I was having too much fun, but later I did. Especially towards my father, not really towards my brothers. Our parents are very involved with us, and they don't hold back on criticism. 'Skill' is also important in our family, but my three brothers are much more virtuosic in that regard. I have it too, but I just had less interest in it. I also wanted to do my own thing quickly, to start distorting things. I find little joy in making a portrait; I would soon start cutting it up and collaging it. My father enjoys that more. His realistic nude drawings are much further from my work.

HVC: Do you ask your brothers for advice, or is there healthy competition?

RV: There is healthy competition. We have points of intersection in our work, but in a different way, we are all very different. Sometimes it's annoying: 'brother of, son of, family of…'

HVC: Do you think so? It can also be an advantage, right?

RV: Maybe it is indeed an advantage that the name is known and that you are therefore not easily forgotten.

HVC: Have you never wanted to do something completely different?

RV: You do question that from time to time. An art historian would also suit me. I also enjoy organizing things, like workshops or a traveling exhibition. I really don't need to be in my studio 7 days a week. That's why I also enjoy teaching and doing many other things in between. But the greatest satisfaction always remains making a good piece of work.

HVC: For the opening of the exhibition at Locuslux, I made a drawing with cells that I had worked on for months. When that was hanging on the wall, I was really satisfied and couldn't take my eyes off it; I was actually in love with my own work.

HVC: Where do you get your inspiration from? From other artists, from nature, from love?

RV: Mainly from life, from the tangible, from the things I see and feel. Not from the rational, although I don't always work purely from instinct.

HVC: Do you work more from instinct or from your head?

RV: I think I work very instinctively, even though I don't live that way; I tend to think ten times before making a decision about something. Just because my formal language seems so organic, it doesn't mean that everything just flows out instinctively: I am very much concerned with how an image works best, that's my rational side. Because in my studio, I never get into a long trance. At Lineart, I also saw two older, stunning paintings by Philippe Vandenberg. I can be jealous of that: that directness, expressiveness, but I could never work that way myself.

HVC: Do you also have no need to integrate living beings into your work?

RV: No, although I do want my work to become a living being, without showing a head or hand or leg. I like to evoke something mental in the viewer. I want you to feel my presence in my work. Although I also know that this can be off-putting, that it can become too much. I don't want it to just blend into the space, merely as a product. That's why I still do many things myself, like painting, because I want you to feel that 'labor' in it. If I only had an idea, it wouldn't satisfy me to have someone else execute it. If you looked at a work by my grandfather, you also felt that there was a strong presence.

HVC: Do you feel painting and working with your hands as a necessary urge? If you haven't painted for a while, would you feel that in something? Do you feel a Sturm und Drang then?

RV: I feel that when I've been teaching too much for a period and I feel that I want to do things myself. That balance always has to be there, even though I don't breathe art 7/7. Although, when I walk, I do have my own way of looking and I notice that I constantly observe things and let them seep in. Although I don't feel that I have to perform and that I have to finish this or that within a week. I even find that a strange sentence: 'I am an artist.' I always say that I am involved in visual art.

HVC: If you're feeling down, is it possible to express yourself 100% in your art?

RV: That has nothing to do with each other. What I can do is let everything go after a busy day by drawing, but I can also release my stress in football; that's even more aggressive, I can shout again. (laughs)

HVC: Does it take a lot of effort for you to make art? Does it sometimes happen that you go to your studio reluctantly? Or can you sit on a terrace all day without feeling guilty?

RV: I do have a great sense of guilt. A day of doing nothing is actually not possible. It's both tension and relaxation. Sometimes you just have to get into it, and before you know it, you roll into a drawing and get a work rush. But it's not like I go to my studio from 9 to 5.

HVC: Is your studio the place where you feel the happiest?

RV: I can say that I have felt very happy in my studio among all my clutter.

HVC: You probably have it in you that even if you didn't have exhibitions, you would still draw and paint. But I suspect your work only exists because of the viewer?

RV: Yes, that's the confirmation I need. I don't work towards an exhibition, but it's a fact that I work more purposefully and concentrated then. You form your exhibition in your mind, and that gives you extra motivation.

HVC: Stopping drawing and painting is not an option?

RV: No. In any case, I will always do something in the creative sphere; I will always need to do something myself, to produce something. I need to create something that comes from me. I do look up to the Sturm und Drang of a Fred Bervoets for whom painting is something vital. For myself, it's also like that, although I don't experience it as extremely.

HVC: At the end of your life, do you want hundreds of works by Robin Vermeersch hanging around? Or would you prefer to keep them to yourself?

RV: I'm not really concerned with that. Some works I would prefer to keep for myself because I love them so much. But the more my work reaches people, the better. I don't want them to end up in boxes in my attic. The good works should be seen. I just work towards the next exhibition, and then I also do it for the contacts and the reactions from people. But my ego is not big enough to want to see my name everywhere.

HVC: If you could return as an artist, who would you want to incarnate as?

RV: I am especially fascinated by the romantic, like the lives of the Impressionists. The idea that they met each other in cafés in the evenings. I feel that everything was more susceptible back then. I would like to be part of such a movement. Maybe I am very romantic about that. We now live in a very individualistic society, and as an artist, you are almost a little business that has to sell itself through catalogs. You have to be very involved in that, but I don't feel at home in it. You also have to fight for your work to be in the best places, but I find all of that so pragmatic. That has always been the case, but with a romantic view, you can glorify the lives of early artists. I partly entered visual art for the romantic idea. The books of Picasso, the correspondence between the Van Gogh brothers... Just as I went with my parents to exhibitions everywhere, it fascinates me to go to various places with my works.

So keep making art!
