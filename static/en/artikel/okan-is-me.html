Okan-is-me

by Jakob de Smaele
AI translation

I can imagine few places as unpleasant as the toilet on a train. You need not be claustrophobic or germ-averse to feel a sense of confinement there. It is a place I most prefer to avoid. When the need is high and I nonetheless step into the tiny cubicle, its walls covered in tags and stickers, while I fly across the Belgian railways at a speed of 200 kilometres an hour, I want to get out again as quickly as possible. That is probably true for you too. But not for the artist Okan Mentes. This nineteen-year-old from Brussels knows how to find beauty even in the ugliest places. Thus in this exhibition there is a photograph he took of the water being sucked cyclone-like into the depths through the stainless-steel bottom of the train toilet bowl. This may sound like a joke – a kind of Duchamp-esque stunt – but it is not. Okan did not take the photograph with the intention of making a conceptual artwork of it. He took the photograph solely because he saw something aesthetic. And that this aesthetic lay in the bottom of a toilet bowl is, in fact, irrelevant.

Artists are not known for being modest. I can assure you that Okan is an exception in this. When I first met him a few weeks ago, I was surprised by his infectious enthusiasm and eagerness to learn. He seemed well aware that, as a young artist, he still had much to learn. I see that modesty reflected in his artworks too. This exhibition is certainly no loud declaration of a coherent philosophy. It is rather an uncertain search for fragmented beauties.

When we behold a terracotta sculpture, we are curious about whose fingerprints are in the clay. Although photographs leave no literal impressions of the artist behind, we are just as curious about what the maker put of themselves into their art. Even the greatest adherent of "the death of the author" cannot switch off this instinctive curiosity. Yet this is exactly what I would ask of you. When looking at Okan's work, try for a moment to forget Okan and to behold the work for what it is. Likewise, you may feel the need to work out what the photographs depict. But as mentioned earlier, whether a photograph was taken of a traffic sign, a paving stone, or even a toilet bowl, is in fact irrelevant. You should actually even forget that the artworks you behold are photographs. Try for a moment to set aside the who, what, where and when. All that then remains is the pure experience of the black-and-white compositions. And when you let that fully come to you, then, I believe, you understand the true value of these artworks.

It is easy to be charmed by the young Okan. Especially when, in his Brussels accent and with his youthful enthusiasm, he reveals the funny anecdotes behind his artworks. Should you ever end up in such a situation: be vigilant. As a viewer, you are not meant to find the artist in his works. You are meant to lose yourself in them!

— Jakob de Smaele
